What Responder is

Responder is an AI SRE first responder that lives in Slack. It answers questions, investigates alerts, and prepares code — and it keeps a full record of how it reached every answer. This page is the whole mental model. Five minutes, four ideas.

Status

Design-partner preview. It runs in production on the author’s own infrastructure. It assumes an operator who is comfortable with a Unix host, a YAML file, and a systemd unit.

The shape of it

Responder runs as one Go binary on one host you control. It connects to Slack over socket mode, so it needs no inbound port. It reads your repositories to learn what your systems are supposed to look like. It checks that against live evidence through Emisar, the infrastructure authority. Then it says what it found.

It talks to a model through Coop, which owns the isolated session and the fork the model works in. You bring your own model and your own account. Responder has no second model runtime and no direct provider client.

Three layers, three owners, and the split is the point:

ConcernOwner
Slack identity, correlation, the durable record, deliveryResponder
Repository allowlist, model target, budgets, the fork and the boxCoop
Infrastructure identity, policy, approval, execution, auditEmisar
Merge, signing, deploymentA person, outside all three

Idea one: everything is an episode

Every accepted unit of work is an episode. A question in a thread is an episode. An alert that fires at 3am is an episode. A scheduled morning health check is an episode. There is no second path and no lightweight path.

An episode owns four things:

Goals
What outcome was requested, and the observable condition that would mean it is done.
Attempts
One model execution each. An episode that was refused and retried has several.
Context manifests
An immutable record of exactly what context each attempt was given, and what was left out.
An event stream
Append-only. Every visible state — working, blocked, waiting for approval, verifying — is a projection of it, not a flag somebody set.

An episode moves through states you can name: accepted, acknowledged, planning, working, blocked, waiting_operator, waiting_external, waiting_approval, retrying, verifying, and then one terminal state. Because the stream is the truth, an episode survives a restart. It parks between turns and picks the same conversation back up.

Every episode has a full trace on the control plane you run yourself: the exact model input, each context layer with why it was included or left out, what the budget trimmed, and every answer the host refused. See the control plane.

Idea two: answers are assembled from evidence

Responder does not write a paragraph and then look for support. It records observations, and the answer is built from what it recorded.

Each evidence row carries its claim (what was being checked), its observation (what was seen), its source (where it came from — repository, Emisar, monitoring, Slack), its observed-at time, its freshness, and its confidence. Confidence is one of high, medium, or low, and the weakest row wins: a claim is only high-confidence when every row behind it is.

A row can also contradict a claim rather than support it. When two sources disagree, Responder quotes both, with their identifiers and their observation times, and asks you to decide. It does not silently prefer the convenient one.

Coverage is reported layer by layer: change, host, runtime, workload, dependency, application, and impact — with hardware, scheduler and task added when the work calls for them. Each layer gets one of healthy, degraded, unhealthy, unknown, or not_applicable.

The rule that matters most

“Missing evidence alone is not degradation, and healthy infrastructure alone does not prove application health.”

A layer nobody could check is unknown, and it says so. It is not quietly called healthy, and it is not alarmingly called degraded. A verdict may only be degraded or unhealthy when a recorded observation carries that effect.

For an operational health question the verdict is healthy, degraded, or unhealthy. Other kinds of work have their own verdict sets — a change review ends succeeded, failed, needs_review, in_progress, or inconclusive; a factual question ends confirmed, not_confirmed, or inconclusive. A question that has no verdict is not forced to invent one.

An episode ends either decision_ready or blocked. A blocked ending must name its blocker from a fixed list — source_unavailable, access_denied, operator_input_required, authority_boundary, tool_failure, capability_unavailable — along with what it tried and what happens next. A partial blocker is rejected whole. “Somebody should query the metrics” is unfinished work, not a blocker, and the host sends it back.

Idea three: the host checks the model’s answer

The model’s answer is an input, not a result. Before anyone sees it, the host parses it against a hard contract and can refuse it. A refused answer goes back to the model with an exact correction and the episode tries again.

There are four kinds of refusal:

unreadable
It did not parse at all.
incomplete
It parsed, but the host would not accept it — a missing verdict, an unexplained coverage row, a cause with no evidence behind it.
rejected
The conclusion was fine, but something attached to it — an offer, a proposed grant — could not be accepted.
shape
Right content, wrong form: too long, or handing the question back instead of answering it.

Every refusal is written to the audit log with the exact correction text, and shown on the episode page. Nothing about this is hidden from you: a bad first answer is part of the record, not something scrubbed out of it.

Then the part that compounds. A refusal becomes a fixture candidate. You keep it or discard it from App Home or the control plane. A kept one is appended to a corpus of recorded real model answers that replay offline as ordinary tests — no credentials, no model call, about a second. The bug that reached production last week is the test that runs on every commit this week.

Idea four: the autonomy ladder

Responder does not have one volume knob. For every message it sees, it picks a rung:

Ignore
Routine noise, chatter, a duplicate, a success notification. The kind of message a teammate would read and say nothing about. The decision is still recorded.
React
One emoji. When a reply would be noise but silence would be rude.
Reply
An evidence-backed answer, posted where the person was speaking.
Incident
A dedicated room. Only from a credible unresolved app alert, or an explicit human request.

The model scores each message on four dimensions — urgency, confidence, novelty, ownership — from 0 to 3 each, and classifies who it was addressed to. The host adds them up and applies your configured thresholds. Replying takes more than reacting. Higher thresholds make it quieter.

Score alone is never enough. An ambient reply must also be material, and must be one of: a correction, new evidence, a decision, a completed action, or a necessary question. Two people talking to each other can only be interrupted by a material correction. A message addressed to a specific human is never reacted to at all.

Human messages do not become incidents on their own. Responder answers in place and may attach an Open incident room button; only a configured operator can press it.

What it cannot do

This list is a feature. Each line is a boundary the host enforces, not an unfinished corner.

  • It cannot merge, deploy from repository changes, or sign a commit.
  • It cannot run a free-form command on a host. There is no shell path. Actions are typed and discovered from a trusted catalog.
  • It cannot approve its own action, act past an approval, or repeat a mutation while verifying one.
  • It cannot widen its own authority. A proposed promotion that skips a rung, or exceeds what the host itself counted, is refused.
  • It cannot push an arbitrary branch, open a non-draft pull request, or publish changes to a companion repository.
  • It cannot write memory, a preference, or a standing rule. It can only propose one for a person to confirm.
  • It cannot discard dirty work. Not through cleanup, not through Slack, not through the control plane.
  • It cannot hide a failed check. A refusal, a stale source, and an unverified layer all appear in the answer.
