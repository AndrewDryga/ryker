The first responder that shows its work.

Responder is an AI teammate for on-call and infrastructure teams. It lives in Slack, watches your alerts, and investigates with real evidence. Every claim names its source. Every risky action asks a person first.

Read the docs Become a design partner View on GitHub

It would rather say “I could not verify this” than guess.

What Responder is built on

01

Answers built from evidence

Every answer is assembled from recorded observations: what was checked, where, and when. If Responder cannot verify a claim, it says so plainly instead of guessing.

02

Power only with permission

There is no production shell. Responder discovers typed actions from a trusted catalog. Policy decides what runs and what waits for a person. Every action is audited. It cannot merge, deploy, or skip an approval.

03

An AI held to a standard

The host checks every model answer against hard rules before anyone sees it. Weak answers are refused and corrected. Real failures become permanent tests. It gets better, on the record.

Four things happen, in this order, every time.

There is no other path. A scheduled check, a webhook, and a question typed at 3am all become the same kind of work, with the same record behind it.

Step 01

It watches

Responder joins your alert and ops channels. It knows when to stay silent, when one emoji is enough, when to answer, and when an alert deserves an incident room.

Step 02

It investigates

It reads the topology declared in your repositories and checks it against live evidence: hosts, workloads, dependencies, application behavior, and impact. Layer by layer.

Step 03

It answers

The verdict comes first — healthy, degraded, or unhealthy — then what it means, the evidence behind it, and the next useful step. Missing evidence is named, never papered over.

Step 04

It remembers

It recalls how similar incidents ended, what deployed recently, and what your team told it. Always as history to check. Never as proof.

Everything Responder does

One honest line each. Nothing here is a roadmap item; if it is listed, it is in the binary you can clone today.

In Slack — 16

The whole surface an on-call engineer touches.

  • Mention answers in place. Ask in a channel or thread; the answer lands there, with no incident created.
  • Thread-aware reads. The thread root, the replies nearest the message, and the channel above the thread.
  • The watch ladder. Ignore, react, reply, incident — one rung chosen per message, with separate thresholds.
  • Alert triage with typed assessments. Verdict, impact, cause, immediate action, verification, and long-term fix.
  • Incident rooms. One channel per occurrence, private by default, with a pinned investigation card that updates in place.
  • Engineering tasks in isolated forks. A “Start task” button turns a request into writable work in the same thread.
  • Host-rendered records. Timeline, evidence, handoff, and postmortem, derived from stored facts rather than prose.
  • Delta updates on repeat alerts. A reply about a still-firing alert is held to what changes the decision, not the whole story again.
  • Closure for cleared alerts. A short close that links the alert it closes. Nothing at all for noise.
  • Guided channel setup. One-click safe defaults, or four questions covering listening, repository, alerts, and audience.
  • App Home and the agent view. Open work, incidents, memory, and suggested prompts, without typing a command.
  • A message shortcut. “Investigate message” runs a read-only investigation even where listening is off.
  • The /responder recovery command. Status, proactive, shadow, assignments, help — what has to work when nothing else does.
  • Reactions as acknowledgement. One emoji when a reply would be noise but silence would be rude.
  • Charts and visuals on request. Bounded, evidence-backed images returned into the same conversation.
  • Feedback capture. React to a reply, or just say what was wrong. It is stored for review and never fed back into a prompt.

Investigation and memory — 12

What it knows, where it got it, and how long that is good for.

  • An evidence ledger. Each row carries its claim, source, observation time, freshness, and confidence.
  • Supersession. A newer observation replaces an older one on the record instead of quietly overwriting it.
  • Layered coverage. Host, runtime, workload, dependency, application, and impact — each reported separately.
  • Recall of resolved past episodes. Up to three, ranked by alert group, shared services, and symptom overlap.
  • A change ledger. Deploys, merges, and applies inside the change window, offered as correlation to check.
  • Thread and channel conversation memory. Purpose, goal, open loops, decisions, and unresolved questions.
  • Related-conversation summaries. Continuity across channels, with private rooms held behind a membership proof.
  • Contradiction handling. When two sources disagree, it quotes both rather than picking the convenient one.
  • Operator-confirmed preferences. Typed defaults such as check depth and reply detail, resolved by scope.
  • Standing read-only rules. Typed channel subscriptions that show exact scope, expiry, and safety boundary before saving.
  • A memory review queue. Keep, merge, or forget. Nothing edits confirmed memory silently.
  • Session rotation that carries memory. A long-running channel starts a fresh session without losing its summary.

Governed action — 10

The parts that can change something, and the gates in front of them.

  • Typed Emisar actions. Discovered from trusted catalogs. No free-form command ever reaches a host.
  • Re-validation at the edge. An outbound-only runner re-checks action, arguments, limits, and pack hash on the target.
  • Approvals owned and audited. Slack shows a link to the exact pending request; the decision is made in Emisar.
  • Runbook drafts. A verified fix can be offered to Emisar as an unpublished runbook draft. Emisar decides if it is ever published.
  • Grant promotions. One rung at a time, earned by verified successes the host counts itself.
  • Standing assignments. Five change classes, a daily budget, an expiry — and shadow only, for now.
  • Schedules. Recurring and one-time checks that produce full episodes, in your timezone, never overlapping.
  • Waits on external events. CI checks, deployments, Terraform runs, and approvals, with durable wakeups.
  • Draft-PR publication. The pushed tree must match the reviewed tree exactly, or it is not published.
  • Verified discard plans. Cleanup touches only sessions Responder recorded, and never dirty or unpublished work.

Platform and trust — 13

What you run, and what you can check afterwards.

  • An episode kernel. Attempts, goals, context manifests, and replay fingerprints, on one append-only stream.
  • A full-trace control plane. The exact model input, every context layer with its why and why-not, and every trim.
  • Refusals on the record. When the host rejects an answer, the rejection and the exact correction sent are both kept.
  • Corrections become fixtures. A kept correction turns into a permanent regression test that replays with no model.
  • Decisions and findings queues. The work the machine owes a human, in one list, with the evidence attached.
  • Model routing profiles. Bring your own model through Coop, with an escalation ladder across providers.
  • One Go binary, one SQLite file. No cluster, no broker, no second datastore.
  • Slack socket mode. No inbound ports required. The service listens on loopback.
  • Bounded webhooks. Grafana and mapped JSON, size-capped, authenticated, deduplicated by delivery identity.
  • Automatic repository mirrors. Declared by slug, kept current in the background, and never modified.
  • Restart-safe. It parks between turns, resumes the same conversation, and survives a process restart.
  • Retention with a long tail. Message bodies expire in a day; episode history and audit are kept far longer.
  • A watchdog. Because the failure no test catches is the one where the machine simply stopped.
Trust· what remains

Trust · what remains

Built to be doubted

Responder assumes you will check its work — and makes checking easy.

  • Every evidence row carries its source and its observed-at time.
  • Every episode keeps its exact model input, fingerprinted for replay.
  • The host refuses answers that break its rules, and shows you the refusal — on a control plane you run yourself.
  • Silence is a feature: cleared alerts get a short closure, noise gets nothing.

Where your data goes

Your prompts go to one place: the model provider you configure, reached through Coop. There is no second model runtime and no direct provider client anywhere in the binary. Slack messages, evidence, episodes, and traces live in one SQLite file on your host. Archived prompts have every credential this process knows replaced with [REDACTED], and a contributor task box is never handed shared tools or environment secrets.

What it cannot do

  • Merge or deploy
  • Run untyped commands in production
  • Act past its approval
  • Widen its own authority
  • Hide a failed check

These are not missing features. Each one is a boundary the host enforces, and each one is the reason the rest can be trusted.
